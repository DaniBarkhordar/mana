01 — NameKelvo, Kelbrook, Mananu

Kelvo had to be withdrawn. trykelvo.com went live in mid-August as a pre-launch GLP-1 supplement brand — your Class 5 — with an open robots.txt asking to be indexed. Nobody asks to be indexed for a domain they're squatting. The rest of the namespace was already gone and KELVO LTD was at Companies House.

Kelbrook was coined and screened as a replacement and came back clean: zero US records, one unrelated UK mark, domains free. It remains the fallback.

Then the whole original list was screened — all 44 names, through the same protocol that caught Kelvo. Thirty-six were dead, ten of them to filings or incorporations from the previous 21 months in exactly this category: Revora (a biomarker-and-supplement app, filed April 2026), Auvra (vitamin gummies, March 2026), Voom Nutrition Ltd (incorporated March 2026), Withings filing Nutrio in Class 9 and 10 last August. Three were legally viable. One was also commercially sound.

Chosen

Mananu. Your coinage. Three open syllables — anyone who hears it once spells it right. Abstract, which is what a platform name has to be: Oura, Whoop, Zoe and Withings are all meaningless words, and Mananu carries a blood test as easily as it carries a scale.

02 — VisionFour stages, four regulatory lines

The stated vision is ZOE's model, Withings' hardware breadth and WHOOP's data moat combined: scales, then a wearable, then blood tests, then supplements chosen against the person's own data. It is a good vision and it stages cleanly — but each stage crosses a line the previous one did not.

The one decision that makes stages 2–4 cheap

Store every measurement — a scale reading, a night's HRV, a ferritin result — as one kind of row: kind, value, unit, source, time, reference range. A cut-down FHIR Observation. A lab partner's API then maps onto your schema in an afternoon, and the trend chart that already works for body fat works for ferritin without new code. This is now Phase 1 of the hand-off.

Don't build a band for launch. The scale factory doesn't make them, white-label bands are poor, and a poor band next to a good scale is the review that gets quoted. The customer who buys a £150 scale bundle very often already owns an Oura, a Garmin or a WHOOP — let them connect it in thirty seconds and you hold the data WHOOP alone can't.

03 — CommercialThe exclusivity is worth more than the discount

Sophia's message says Scanfit holds exclusive distribution in Denmark, Norway, Sweden, Holland, Finland and the United States.

Read that list for what isn't on it. The UK isn't. Nor is Germany, France, Spain, Italy, Ireland or Poland. The largest markets in Europe are unclaimed on this product — and Scanfit, the incumbent, is a small Danish operation sitting at 2.8 stars on Google Play with around 5,000 Android installs.

First ask

Not the $200 coupon. Exclusive UK and Ireland distribution, with right of first refusal on Germany, France, Spain and Italy. If they resist on a first order — normal — ask for exclusivity triggered by volume: non-exclusive now, converting once you pass an agreed unit count. That costs them nothing and gives you a path. Get it on the proforma invoice, not in a chat.

Without that clause you'll be competing on Amazon UK against three other people buying the same white-label unit from the same factory within a year.

One more thing worth knowing: you don't need the final name to place the order. Most factories will run a first batch on neutral packaging and hold the printed carton until you send artwork. Ask — it decouples the trademark work from the production slot.

04 — ProductWhere the accuracy actually goes

A controlled-feeding study presented at NUTRITION 2026 measured the leading calorie apps against 102 standardised meals weighed to a tenth of a gram. All four underestimated, by 250–345 kcal per meal on average, with fat under-reported by roughly 30 g.

Break that error into its three sources, and it's clear what a scale can and can't fix:

Identification Quantity Cooking fat & preparation
Identification — what is it. The camera is good at this. Quantity — the scale solves it completely. Cooking fat — invisible to any camera. The biggest share.

So the honest pitch isn't "our scale is accurate". It's that the scale eliminates the quantity error outright, and the app attacks the largest remaining one with a workflow no photo app can copy: weigh the pan, weigh the oil, weigh what's left. The difference went into the food.

What that looks like in the app

  • Running tare. Add one ingredient at a time; the app captures each one from the change in weight, so the bowl is never emptied. Each capture is an exact food-and-grams pair.
  • Cooking-oil capture. The line item that targets the published failure mode directly.
  • Raw-to-cooked conversion. Logging 225 g of cooked rice against a dry database row overstates it by 534 kcal. The app converts.
  • Personal calibration. Because you hold true grams for foods this user eats repeatedly, you can measure the model's bias for them and correct it. A photo-only app has no ground truth and can never close that loop.
  • A "% weighed" figure on every day and every meal. Simultaneously the honesty statement and the retention loop — people want to raise a percentage, and the only way to raise this one is to use the scale.

Keeping the AI near-free

Four decisions, in order of impact. Never ask the model for portion size — that's the whole point of owning a scale, and it means a smaller model and fewer output tokens. Downscale to 512 px on the device before upload. Cache by image hash, shared across users, because people photograph the same breakfast for weeks. And send context — time of day, locale, recent foods — which a published benchmark found cuts calorie error more than a model upgrade would.

Net effect: well under a tenth of a penny per identification, roughly a hundredth of what a specialist food-recognition API charges. Metering exists to stop a runaway client loop, not because the calls are expensive — and when the quota runs out, weighing still works.

Every numeric claim in the engine is covered by unit tests written from the published coefficients, and re-checked independently by a second implementation — 40 checks, all passing. The app runs today on a simulated driver, so screens can be built and App Review can be given a route through the flow without hardware.

Two decisions in the code worth knowing about

There is no visceral fat rating and no metabolic age. Visceral fat from a foot-to-foot scale has no published algorithm and no validation; metabolic age is a restatement of BMR against a population table. Bone mass is computed, and labelled "not measured", because bone mineral is effectively invisible to a 50 kHz current — it moves with your weight, and users read that as bone change.

The vendor SDK sits behind an interface. It's a closed binary needing an encrypted licence blob to start. Keeping it behind ScaleDriver means a lapsed licence doesn't brick the product, and a second-source scale is a new adapter rather than a rewrite.

The GDPR trap in the factory's SDK

Their SDK offers two paths for the body-composition maths: a local library, and a cloud endpoint at uniquehealth.lefuenergy.com. The cloud endpoint takes age, sex, height, weight, heart rate and impedance — Article 9 special-category health data — to a server in the PRC. No adequacy decision, no SCCs, no processor agreement. Their own consumer app's privacy policy says PRC data stays in the PRC and mentions GDPR nowhere.

A privacy notice does not fix that. The app is architected so it never happens: BLE, to the phone, to local calculation, to your EU backend. Ask them for a DPA anyway.
